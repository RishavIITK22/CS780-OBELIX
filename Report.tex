\begin{abstract}
I studied the OBELIX box-pushing task as a partially observable control problem with three latent behaviours: find, push, and unwedge. The development path moved from a Double-DQN baseline to PPO with hand-crafted belief states, then to paper-inspired modular policies. The best model was a three-policy recurrent PPO controller: GRU policies for finding and pushing, and an LSTM policy for unwedging, all using raw 18-bit observations and light behaviour-specific shaping.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Problem Description}
OBELIX is a simulated warehouse robot that must find a grey box, attach to it, push it to the arena boundary, and then recover from wedged states. The action set is discrete:
\[
\mathcal{A}=\{\text{L45},\text{L22},\text{FW},\text{R22},\text{R45}\}.
\]
The observation is an 18-bit vector: 16 sonar bits from near/far bins around the robot, one infrared contact bit, and one stuck bit. The conceptual task order is
\[
\text{find}\rightarrow \text{attach}\rightarrow \text{push}\rightarrow \text{unwedge},
\]
but this phase is not directly given to the agent.

The benchmark has three increasing difficulty levels: a static box, a blinking box, and a moving plus blinking box. Wall obstacles add a narrow-passage navigation problem and make false push/unwedge decisions costly. The problem is a POMDP: blank sonar frames do not imply that the box is absent, the IR bit is not a persistent attachment flag, and the same 18-bit observation can require different actions depending on recent contacts. The goal was therefore not just high local reward, but robustness across hidden seeds, blinking, motion, and walls.

I treated the hidden state as
\[
s_t=(x_t^{\mathrm{bot}},y_t^{\mathrm{bot}},\theta_t^{\mathrm{bot}},
x_t^{\mathrm{box}},y_t^{\mathrm{box}},v_t^{\mathrm{box}},
\text{visibility}_t,\text{attachment}_t,\text{wall contacts}_t),
\]
while the policy only receives \(o_t\in\{0,1\}^{18}\). This mismatch is important: two episodes can have the same \(o_t=\mathbf{0}\), but in one episode the box was last seen to the left and in another it was last seen to the right. A memoryless policy must map both to the same action, whereas a recurrent policy can condition on the observation history \(h_t=(o_0,a_0,\ldots,o_t)\).

\section{Approach and Model Evolution}
\subsection*{Phase 1: Double-DQN Baseline}
The first phase targeted difficulty 0. I implemented a Double-DQN baseline in \texttt{train\_ddqn.py}, with a two-layer MLP \(18\!\to\!64\!\to\!64\!\to\!5\), replay buffer, target network, and \(\epsilon\)-greedy exploration \citep{mnih2015dqn,vanhasselt2016double}. The Bellman target was
\[
y_t=r_t+\gamma Q_{\theta^-}(s_{t+1},\arg\max_a Q_{\theta}(s_{t+1},a)).
\]
I also implemented \texttt{vec\_env.py} to run multiple OBELIX environments in parallel through subprocesses. This improved data throughput and made it easier to compare reward shaping variants. Reward shaping at this stage was intentionally minimal: reward scaling, a small living cost, and the environment reward signal.

This baseline was useful because it established two facts early. First, parallel sampling was necessary; single-environment training produced noisy curves and slow iteration. Second, value bootstrapping alone did not solve the long-horizon credit assignment problem. The replay buffer contained many blank-observation transitions and only a few terminal-success transitions, so the learned \(Q\)-values often preferred short spinning patterns over risky forward exploration.

\subsection*{Phase 2: Monolithic PPO with Belief Features}
For difficulty 2, I moved to PPO because the blinking box made off-policy value learning brittle \citep{schulman2017ppo}. The first PPO variants used \texttt{state\_encoder.py} and \texttt{reward\_shaper.py}. The encoder built a stacked belief vector from raw bits, sector strengths, direction summaries, temporal counters, transition cues, and the previous action. The shaper rewarded front/side evidence during finding, forward progress during pushing, and recovery from stuck states.

The PPO objective was the clipped surrogate
\[
L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_t\left[
\min\left(\rho_t A_t,\operatorname{clip}(\rho_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A_t\right)
\right],
\quad
\rho_t=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t|o_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_t|o_t)}.
\]
I submitted PPO frame stacking and PPO LSTM variants \citep{hochreiter1997lstm}. Locally, the agent sometimes completed episodes, but Codabench rewards were on the order of \(-10^5\). Removing the handcrafted encoder and most shaping improved robustness. The raw-observation variants still spun frequently, solved only occasionally, and were weak near walls.

The main lesson of Phase 2 was that local visual success was not a sufficient criterion. The frame-stacked policy could exploit stable local rollouts, but hidden evaluation seeds changed the distribution of blank intervals and wall contacts. The LSTM variant had a better information structure than frame stacking, but a single monolithic policy still had to learn three qualitatively different behaviours from one reward stream.

\subsection*{Phase 3: Paper-Inspired Three-Policy PPO}
The original OBELIX paper uses a behaviour-based decomposition where unwedging suppresses pushing, and pushing suppresses finding \citep{mahadevan1991obelix}. I replicated this idea in \texttt{train\_ppo\_three\_policy.py}: three independent PPO policies, a fixed behaviour manager, and priority
\[
\text{unwedge} \succ \text{push} \succ \text{find}.
\]
I tried the belief encoder and then raw observations, with minimal shaping and a find-only anti-spinning penalty from \texttt{anti\_spin\_penalty.py}. The modular design was conceptually cleaner, but the actual performance remained poor. The find policy still found spinning attractors, and walls produced many stale IR or stuck transitions that confused the push/unwedge switch.

The failure was not that the hierarchy was wrong; it was that each module was still too reactive. If the find module received a blank observation after seeing the box, it had no internal record of the last useful direction. If the push module saw IR flicker, it could drop contact too early. If the unwedge module saw repeated stuck frames, it could learn a symmetric turn policy that did not distinguish which direction would clear the wall.

\subsection*{Final Phase: Recurrent Three-Policy PPO}
I then extended the three-policy idea through several experiments. The goal of this phase was to keep the useful behaviour decomposition, but fix two recurring problems: blank-observation memory loss and unreliable phase switching.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Latent three-policy PPO.} In \texttt{train\_ppo\_three\_policy\_latent.py}, I tried replacing hand-coded observation summaries with learned recurrent latent beliefs. Each behaviour had a recurrent actor-critic similar to
    \[
    z_t=f_\phi(o_t),\qquad h_t^b=\mathrm{LSTM}_b([z_t,a_{t-1},r_{t-1},d_{t-1}],h_{t-1}^b),
    \]
    and the policy was \(\pi_b(a_t|h_t^b)\). I also experimented with curiosity for the find phase, using prediction errors such as
    \[
    r_t^{\mathrm{int}}\propto \left\|\hat{z}_{t+1}-z_{t+1}\right\|_2^2,
    \qquad
    r_t^{\mathrm{train}}=r_t^{\mathrm{env}}+\eta r_t^{\mathrm{int}}.
    \]
    The motivation was that the find policy needed exploration when all sensors were blank. In practice this performed poorly, about \(-2\times10^5\) on Codabench. The intrinsic reward made exploration broader, but it also rewarded novelty unrelated to pushing the box out, and the recurrent latent state was hard to train with sparse terminal success.

    \item \textbf{Explicit Phase Belief (EPB).} In \texttt{train\_ppo\_epb.py}, I moved in the opposite direction: instead of asking an RNN to discover all memory, I built a compact persistent belief \(b_t\). The key update was non-decaying memory:
    \[
    d_t=
    \begin{cases}
    \phi(o_t), & \text{if any box sensor/IR evidence is visible},\\
    d_{t-1}, & \text{if }o_t\text{ is blank},
    \end{cases}
    \]
    where \(d_t\) stores last useful direction/contact evidence. The policy input was \(x_t=[o_t,b_t,\phi_{\mathrm{FSM}}(o_t,b_t)]\). I used a dual-head actor-critic with a soft gate:
    \[
    g_t=\sigma(w_g^\top f_\theta(x_t)),\qquad
    \ell_t=(1-g_t)\ell_t^{\mathrm{find}}+g_t\ell_t^{\mathrm{push}},
    \]
    plus a gate loss \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{gate}}=\operatorname{BCE}(g_t,\texttt{enable\_push}_t)\) during training. The motivation was to prevent GRU-style memory washout over hundreds of blank steps and to make phase switching differentiable. This improved to roughly \(-1.5\times10^4\), but the hand-coded belief still made mistakes near walls and could overcommit to stale direction estimates.

    \item \textbf{Hybrid residual DDQN.} In \texttt{train\_hybrid\_ddqn.py}, I tried a more structured controller. A deterministic graph proposed a small candidate set \(C_t\subseteq\mathcal{A}\) for search/align/push/unwedge, and DDQN learned only the residual choice inside that set:
    \[
    a_t=\arg\max_{a\in C_t}Q_\theta(f_t,a),\qquad
    y_t=r_t+\gamma Q_{\theta^-}\!\left(f_{t+1},\arg\max_{a'\in C_{t+1}}Q_\theta(f_{t+1},a')\right).
    \]
    Here \(f_t\) was a compact feature vector from the hybrid controller. The motivation was pragmatic: if full RL wasted samples on obviously bad actions, restrict the action space and let RL resolve ambiguous choices. This improved further to about \(-1.8\times10^3\), but success was still occasional because a hard rule graph is brittle when walls and blinking create ambiguous sensor events.
\end{itemize}

The final working model is \texttt{ppo\_gru\_un.py}. It keeps the three-policy hierarchy, but uses raw observations and recurrent policies: GRU for find and push \citep{cho2014gru}, and LSTM for unwedge. This matched the POMDP structure better than the previous feed-forward policies while avoiding the over-engineered belief encoder.

\subsection*{Final Model in Detail}
The final policy is a modular recurrent actor-critic. At each time step the raw observation is \(o_t\in\{0,1\}^{18}\). A deterministic priority manager selects one behaviour \(b_t\in\{F,P,U\}\), where \(F\) is find, \(P\) is push, and \(U\) is unwedge:
\[
b_t=
\begin{cases}
U, & \text{if stuck bit is active or unwedge grace is positive},\\
P, & \text{else if IR bit is active or push grace is positive},\\
F, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]
The grace counters are important because the relevant physical event is not perfectly visible in a single frame. The IR bit can flicker during pushing, and the stuck bit can clear one step before the robot has safely rotated away from the wall. I used a push grace of 7 steps, an unwedge grace of 10 steps, and a post-unwedge cooldown of 10 steps to avoid immediately switching back into a wall-triggered push.

Each behaviour has a separate recurrent state and a separate actor-critic network. First the raw observation is embedded:
\[
e_t^b=\tanh(W_e^b o_t + c_e^b).
\]
For find and push I used GRU cores:
\[
z_t^b=\sigma(W_z^b e_t^b+U_z^b h_{t-1}^b),\quad
r_t^b=\sigma(W_r^b e_t^b+U_r^b h_{t-1}^b),
\]
\[
\tilde{h}_t^b=\tanh(W_h^b e_t^b+U_h^b(r_t^b\odot h_{t-1}^b)),\quad
h_t^b=(1-z_t^b)\odot h_{t-1}^b+z_t^b\odot \tilde{h}_t^b,
\quad b\in\{F,P\}.
\]
Find benefits from GRU memory because the robot often receives long blank observations after briefly seeing the box. Push benefits because attachment is not directly observable; the policy must remember recent IR/contact evidence. I used a larger hidden size for find (\(256\)) than push (\(128\)) because find has the broader exploration problem.

For unwedge I used an LSTM:
\[
\begin{aligned}
i_t &= \sigma(W_i e_t^U+U_i h_{t-1}^U),\quad
f_t = \sigma(W_f e_t^U+U_f h_{t-1}^U),\\
g_t &= \tanh(W_g e_t^U+U_g h_{t-1}^U),\quad
o_t^g = \sigma(W_o e_t^U+U_o h_{t-1}^U),\\
c_t^U &= f_t\odot c_{t-1}^U+i_t\odot g_t,\quad
h_t^U=o_t^g\odot\tanh(c_t^U).
\end{aligned}
\]
I chose LSTM for unwedge because this behaviour needs a short but precise memory of the recent stuck trajectory. The cell state helps preserve whether the previous rotation was clearing or worsening the contact, which is useful near walls and corners.

For the selected behaviour \(b_t\), actor and critic heads are
\[
\ell_t^b=W_\pi^b h_t^b+c_\pi^b,\qquad
\pi^b(a_t|o_{\le t})=\operatorname{softmax}(\ell_t^b),\qquad
V^b_t=W_V^b h_t^b+c_V^b.
\]
During training and submission, actions are sampled:
\[
a_t\sim \pi^{b_t}(\cdot|o_{\le t}),
\]
instead of using \(\arg\max_a \pi(a|o_{\le t})\). This kept evaluation consistent with the stochastic PPO policy and avoided prematurely collapsing into repeated turn actions.

The recurrent PPO update stores the recurrent state at the start of each step. For a chunk starting at time \(k\), the update replays \(o_k,\ldots,o_{k+T-1}\) from the stored \(h_k\) or \((h_k,c_k)\), with \(T=16\). Advantages use GAE:
\[
\delta_t=r_t+\gamma V_{t+1}(1-d_t)-V_t,\qquad
A_t=\sum_{l\ge 0}(\gamma\lambda)^l\delta_{t+l}.
\]
The optimized loss for each behaviour is
\[
\mathcal{L}_b
=-\mathbb{E}\left[\min(\rho_t A_t,\operatorname{clip}(\rho_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A_t)\right]
+c_v\mathbb{E}(V_t-R_t)^2-c_e\mathbb{E}\mathcal{H}(\pi_t),
\]
where \(\rho_t=\pi_\theta(a_t|o_{\le t})/\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_t|o_{\le t})\). This preserves PPO's trust-region-like update while allowing gradients to flow through short recurrent sequences.

Reward shaping in the final model was deliberately local and small. For push, forward motion with recent IR evidence and no stuck bit received a bonus; other push actions received a penalty. For unwedge, continued stuck frames received a capped increasing penalty, while clearing both stuck and IR received an escape bonus. The find policy was mostly left on raw rewards, because earlier experiments showed that dense find bonuses produced sensor-chasing and spin loops.

The main empirical pattern was that extra engineered features did not consistently improve leaderboard performance. Locally they often made the policy look better, but Codabench exposed overfitting to sensor summaries and shaped rewards. Raw observations plus recurrent memory was more robust because it let the policy learn its own belief state while preserving the true simulator signal.

Two comparisons were especially informative. First, frame stacking and LSTM both tried to address partial observability, but frame stacking has a fixed memory horizon while the LSTM was trained as one monolithic controller. In contrast, the final model separates the memories by behaviour; the push memory is not overwritten during long find periods, and the unwedge memory is only updated when the stuck-recovery subproblem is active. Second, the hybrid DDQN showed that hand-coded structure can reduce the action search space, but it remained brittle because its graph still had hard cases around wall contacts. The final PPO model kept the useful structure--find, push, unwedge--but learned the action distributions inside each behaviour.

The final result should therefore be interpreted as a design result as much as a score result. The best architecture was not the largest or most feature-rich one. It was the one whose inductive bias matched the simulator: raw local sensors, a small priority hierarchy, and recurrent state where the environment hides information.

\section{Error Analysis and Discussion}
The dominant failure mode in Level 1 was blind spinning: once all sonar bits were zero, a feed-forward policy could converge to repeated turns because each blank observation looked identical. Level 2 added false negatives from blinking. The agent could lose contact with a previously visible box and treat the blank frame as a new search problem. Level 3 added motion, so stale direction estimates became actively misleading. Walls were the hardest case because the wall can trigger sonar, IR, and stuck bits similar to the box; pushing into the wall also creates large negative rewards.

The most surprising result was that the larger handcrafted belief representation was not the best solution. It encoded useful quantities, but it also exposed the policy to shaped statistics that did not always match the hidden evaluation seeds. The modular hierarchy was still useful, but only after adding recurrent state to the active behaviours. The final limitation is that the behaviour manager is still hand-coded. With more time, I would learn the gating policy jointly, add a wall-aware auxiliary prediction task, and run systematic ablations on recurrent hidden size and entropy schedules.

A second limitation is that the final pusher uses an inferred attachment state. The simulator has an internal \texttt{enable\_push} flag, but the submitted policy only sees \(o_t\). My training code uses an IR probe to reduce false pushes, but this is still a heuristic. A cleaner version would learn a latent Bernoulli variable \(z_t^{\mathrm{attach}}\) with an auxiliary loss from simulator labels during training, and then use \(p(z_t^{\mathrm{attach}}|o_{\le t})\) at test time. Similarly, wall discrimination could be framed as an auxiliary prediction problem:
\[
\hat{w}_t = f_\psi(h_t),\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{wall}}=\operatorname{BCE}(\hat{w}_t,w_t),
\]
where \(w_t\) is available during training from simulator geometry but not used by the final policy input.

\section{Conclusion}
The project progressed from a simple Double-DQN baseline to a recurrent modular PPO controller. The strongest lesson was that OBELIX is not just a small discrete-action task; the hidden phase and intermittent sensors make memory essential. The final GRU/GRU/LSTM three-policy PPO retained the behaviour-based insight from the original OBELIX paper while using modern actor-critic training. Future work should focus on learned phase gating, better wall discrimination, and curriculum/evaluation protocols that reduce the gap between local rollouts and Codabench.
